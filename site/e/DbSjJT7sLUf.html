Transcript excerpt
In 1967, a computer scientist called Melvin Conway noticed something strange enough that he wrote it up and sent it to Harvard Business Review. They rejected it on the grounds that he hadn't proved his thesis, so he sent it to a computing magazine instead, and they published it in 68. And the idea became one of the most quietly confirmed observations in how organizations work. Any company that designs a system will produce a design that copies the company's own communication structure, not as a metaphor, but literally. Four teams building a compiler will ship a compiler with four parts. A company where two departments barely speak will ship a product where two components barely connect. The organization chart leaks into the product piece by piece, whether anyone intends it or not. I told the Sony story a while ago, three divisions invading incompatible music players on the same stage, and Conway is the law underneath it. The customer ends up holding a physical copy of your internal politics. The reason is almost obvious, once said out loud. People design what they can talk about, and they can only talk across the channels that exist. If the channel between two teams is thin, the connection between their two components will be also thin and awkward and late. The lens that follows is the sharp part, and modern engineering systems and teams use it deliberately.
