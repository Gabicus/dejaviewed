Thesis

Roots musicians already treat the room as an instrument — testing a 200-year-old house's resonance, filming tunes on porches and in old venues, the way Lomax filmed singers in their own kitchens. A phone can now capture that room as a Gaussian splat, and Gausseous makes the splat react to audio in a browser. Record the room's acoustics in the same session and the sound and the image come from one capture of one place.

Why it matters

A two-person act with a phone gets a music video format where the place itself moves with the tune — no crew, no generated faces, and the result is documentary, not synthetic. It also gives folk and field-recording traditions a native visual form instead of borrowing pop-video tropes.

3 · Step-by-step prompts

Step 1 — Scan the room and measure its sound

Goal: Capture one place as both a clean splat and a measured impulse response, in one session.

At the chosen room (a porch, church, barn or resonant old house like the Girardeau House), do three captures in one session.
(1) Splat scan with Polycam or Luma on a phone. Move slowly in overlapping passes, keep exposure even, and over-cover the area where the performer will stand. Export .ply or .splat. Where phone photogrammetry struggles in low light or large caves, note the @peppes_ghost DIY LiDAR option as a fallback.
(2) Impulse response: play an exponential sine sweep through a speaker and record it with a flat mic at the performer's position. You will deconvolve this into an IR.
(3) Dry performance take: record the instrument and voice close-miked or DI, so the room is not already baked into the sound.
Log the room name, permission, lighting and mic positions.

Expect: capture/ with the .splat, the sweep recording, the dry take, and capture_notes.md covering coverage, lighting and positions.

Audit before moving on: Open the .splat in a viewer. If the performance area has holes or heavy noise, rescan in better light before continuing. Confirm the dry take has minimal room sound. Fail if the IR is only a clap when a sweep was possible, or if no dry take exists.

Step 2 — Deconvolve the impulse response

Goal: Turn the sweep into a usable convolution reverb IR with a measured RT60.

Deconvolve the recorded sweep against the reference sweep to recover the room's impulse response, using Farina's exponential-sine-sweep method. Do it in Python with numpy and scipy, or in Room EQ Wizard.
From the IR, compute the room's RT60 (the time for the energy to decay 60 dB, usually via the T30 or T20 extrapolation from the Schroeder integral).
Save the IR as a WAV ready for a convolution reverb.
Inspect the IR for artifacts: a clean exponential decay, no pre-ringing, no clipping in the sweep recording.
Record the RT60 and the IR length. This measured RT60 is the target the processed audio must match later. Keep the reference sweep file and the recorded sweep together, labeled, so the deconvolution is reproducible. Note the speaker and mic used, since the IR carries their coloration and you may want to compensate.

Expect: capture/room_ir.wav, a plot of the IR and its decay curve, and the measured RT60 value.

Audit before moving on: Check the sweep recording for clipping before trusting the IR. If the decay curve is not roughly linear in dB, the measurement is contaminated, so remeasure. Fail if RT60 cannot be computed or the IR shows pre-ringing from a bad deconvolution.

Step 3 — Convolve the room into the recording

Goal: Make the dry take carry the real room's acoustics and verify the match.

Load the dry performance take into a DAW with a convolution reverb, or convolve in Python (scipy.signal.fftconvolve), using capture/room_ir.wav.
Balance the dry and wet signal so the result sounds like the performance happened in that room, not drowned in reverb.
Then verify: measure the RT60 of the processed take (from a decaying note or by analysis) and compare it with the room's measured RT60 from step 2. They should agree within about 10%.
If you have a wet reference recording of the same performance in the room, compare the convolved version to it as a second check.
Export the processed audio. Keep both the dry and the processed takes, so the edit can choose. Note the wet/dry ratio you settled on, because it is the single biggest taste decision here and you will want to recall it for the next room.

Expect: audio/performance_room.wav and a short match report: processed RT60 versus measured RT60, with the percentage difference.

Audit before moving on: If the processed RT60 differs from the measured room by more than 10%, re-check the wet/dry balance and the IR normalization. Fail if a generic reverb preset was used instead of the measured IR, or if the match report is missing.

Step 4 — Make the splat react to the music

Goal: Load the splat into Gausseous and drive it musically from the audio.

Load the .splat into Gausseous (quixbrics.github.io/gausseous). Confirm it renders without holes in the performance area.
Use the Web Audio AnalyserNode to split the processed audio into bands. Map them deliberately: bass drives overall scale or a slow pulse, mids drive position drift, highs drive shimmer or point size. Gate each mapping with a threshold and smooth it with an attack/release envelope so the motion reads as musical rather than twitchy.
Use a sustained, droning passage, like the khaen or a bowed note, to tune the response, because long tones make reactivity legible.
Add Gausseous's depth of field, a little lens curvature and film emulation for the volumetric glow in the DeepPIV reference, without burying the room.
Export a reactive clip at least as long as the final edit.

Expect: render/ with the Gausseous project or settings, a per-band reactivity map, and an exported reactive clip.

Audit before moving on: Watch the clip against the audio. If the motion looks random or disconnected from the beat, raise the gates and lengthen the smoothing. Confirm no missing-geometry holes show in frame. Fail if the reactivity is unsmoothed strobing or if the splat reveals holes where the performer is.

Step 5 — Cut the 60-second piece

Goal: Edit a vertical cut intercutting real performance and the reacting splat.

Assemble a 60-second vertical (9:16) edit. Intercut real performance footage, honest and documentary in the Lomax spirit, with the reacting splat. The place itself should move with the tune.
Sync the cut to the processed room audio.
Open on something that establishes the real place, so the splat reads as that same room transformed, not a generic effect. Follow the @honeycrush.online proof that a two-person, one-camera shoot can carry a strong mood.
Keep the musicians and the place named in the caption. No generated faces, no synthetic performance.
Export at the platform's preferred vertical resolution and frame rate. Keep the project file and a frame-accurate marker list so re-exports match. Export a square or landscape version too if the act wants it for other platforms, from the same timeline.

Expect: edit/final_60s.mp4, a vertical cut synced to the room audio, with an honest caption naming the place and players.

Audit before moving on: Confirm the edit opens on the real place and that the viewer can tell the splat is that room. Confirm the audio is the convolved room take, not the dry take. Fail if the piece hides the real performance behind effects, or runs long enough to lose a short-form viewer.

Step 6 — A/B against a plain performance video

Goal: Test whether the splat format earns its complexity with the audience.

Make a control: a plain performance video of the same tune in the same room, same length, no splat.
Post the splat cut and the control on the same account, matched for day and time of day, far enough apart not to cannibalize each other, and ideally counterbalanced across two tunes.
Pre-register the metric: average watch-through (percent of the video watched) and completion rate.
Collect the platform analytics for both after a set window (for example 7 days).
State the pass rule in advance: the splat cut holds watch-through at least as well as the plain video. If it loses watch-through, the format is decoration, per the dive's falsifier. Record the view count, likes and shares alongside retention, but judge on retention, since reach depends on the algorithm. Keep the raw analytics screenshots in ab_test/ as evidence for the audit.

Expect: ab_test/ with the control video, the posting log, and a metrics sheet of watch-through and completion for both.

Audit before moving on: Confirm the two posts are matched for time and length, or the comparison is confounded. If total views are too low to distinguish the two, extend the window rather than calling a result. Fail if the metric was chosen after seeing the numbers.

Step 7 — Validate against oracles and write the gap report

Goal: Judge the piece by capture quality, acoustic match and audience retention.

Run the oracle pass:
(a) The .splat opens and reacts in a desktop browser with no holes in the performance area.
(b) The processed RT60 is within 10% of the room's measured RT60.
(c) A blind listening test: people match the processed audio to the room better than chance.
(d) The A/B watch-through and completion of the splat cut versus the control.
(e) The falsifier: are interior scans too noisy to read, or does the splat cut lose watch-through? If either, say the format is a gimmick for this room.
Write report.md with the sections Capture, Acoustics, Render, Edit, A/B Result, Falsifier and Gaps. State plainly whether the format earned its complexity. Rank the gaps by effect on the result.

Expect: report.md, a five-row oracle table, and a ranked gap list.

Audit before moving on: Any acoustic 'match' without the RT60 numbers is downgraded to unverified. Any A/B 'win' without matched posts is downgraded to inconclusive. Confirm the report names the real place and musicians. Fail if the falsifier section is missing.

5 · What proves it right

  • The exported .splat opens and reacts in Gausseous in a desktop browser at an acceptable frame rate, with no holes in the performance area.
  • Acoustic match: the processed take's RT60 is within about 10% of the room's measured RT60 from the impulse response.
  • Blind listening test: people match the processed audio to the correct room better than chance.
  • A/B retention: watch-through and completion of the splat cut versus a matched plain-performance control on the same account.
  • The pre-registered falsifier: interior scans too noisy to read, or the splat cut losing watch-through, means the format is a gimmick for this room.

6 · Failure modes

  • Noisy low-light scan. Tell: holes and floaters where the performer stands. Fix: rescan with even light and overlapping coverage, or fall back to DIY LiDAR for big dark rooms.
  • Fake room sound. Tell: a reverb preset instead of the measured IR. Fix: deconvolve a real sweep and match RT60 within 10%.
  • Baked-in room on the source. Tell: no dry take, so the IR adds reverb on top of reverb. Fix: always capture a dry close-mic or DI take.
  • Twitchy reactivity. Tell: the splat strobes on transients unrelated to the music. Fix: gate and smooth each band, and tune on sustained drones.
  • Effect over substance. Tell: the real place and performance vanish behind filters. Fix: open on the room and keep the documentary core.
  • Confounded A/B. Tell: the two posts went out at different times or lengths. Fix: match posting time and length and pre-register the metric.

7 · Agent direction (unattended runs)

Capture is the bottleneck, so get the room right in one session: a clean splat, a proper sine-sweep IR, and a dry take. A bad scan cannot be fixed later. Cache the .splat, the IR and the dry take under capture/ and never overwrite the originals. Do the deconvolution and convolution in the main thread, since the RT60 match gates everything downstream. Spawn a sub-agent for the Gausseous reactivity tuning, giving it the processed audio and the per-band mapping goals.
